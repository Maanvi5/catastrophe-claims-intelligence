# Current-State Workflow

## Purpose

Map how insurers respond to a major weather catastrophe from pre-event preparation through claims settlement, including the people involved, decisions made, information used, and role of environmental intelligence.

## Scope

Working example: Property insurance and a major weather catastrophe.

The workflow will be examined across:

1. Pre-event preparation
2. Event / immediate response
3. Claims intake and surge
4. Claims triage and prioritisation
5. Assessment and adjustment
6. Settlement

## Research Method

For each stage, we will identify:

- Who owns the decision?
- What decision is being made?
- What information is available?
- What tools or systems are used?
- What environmental information is used?
- What action follows?
- What constraints or pain points exist?

## 1. Pre-Event Preparation

### What happens?

When a significant weather event is developing, insurers monitor the event and assess its potential impact on their insured portfolio. The objective is to anticipate the scale and location of potential losses and prepare the claims operation before claims arrive.

### Key decisions

- Which parts of the insured portfolio may be affected?
- How significant could the event be?
- How many claims might emerge?
- Where might claims be concentrated?
- What claims/response capacity may be required?
- Should additional claims resources be mobilised?

### Who is involved?

- Catastrophe management / CAT response team
- Claims leadership
- Operations / resource planning
- Catastrophe modelling and analytics teams
- Potentially actuarial / reserving teams

### Information used

- Weather forecasts and observations
- Catastrophe / hazard models
- Insured portfolio and customer locations
- Historical and emerging claim trends
- Claims resource availability

### Role of environmental intelligence

Environmental information is already used to monitor developing events and estimate potential impact. Its value comes from being combined with insurer-specific exposure and operational data rather than being used as a standalone weather feed.

### Initial observation

The pre-event workflow is already supported by sophisticated catastrophe modelling and response systems. The opportunity is therefore unlikely to be simply providing insurers with weather forecasts.

## 2. Event / Immediate Response

### What happens?

Once the weather catastrophe occurs, the insurer moves from anticipating potential impact to assessing the actual event and its effect on the insured portfolio.

New information becomes available, including observed weather conditions, hazard/event footprints, satellite or aerial imagery, and early reports of damage.

### Key decisions

- Which areas and insured assets appear to have been affected?
- How large and severe is the impact?
- Where should claims response resources be concentrated?
- How should the expected impact estimate be updated?
- What information should be made available to claims teams and adjusters?

### Who is involved?

- Chief / Head of Claims
- Catastrophe claims management team
- Claims operations
- Loss adjusters / surveyors
- External adjusting partners

### Information used

- Observed weather and hazard data
- Event footprint / affected geography
- Satellite and aerial imagery
- Insured portfolio and property locations
- Early claims / loss reports
- Pre-event impact estimates

### Role of environmental intelligence

Environmental intelligence helps the insurer move from an expected event footprint to an assessment of observed impact. Weather, hazard and imagery data can be combined with insured property information to identify affected areas and properties and support claims response planning.

### Initial observation

The event-response stage already uses multiple environmental and geospatial data sources. Existing solutions can provide portfolio-level impact information and, increasingly, property-level damage assessment.

The potential product opportunity therefore needs to be more specific than simply providing environmental or satellite data.
